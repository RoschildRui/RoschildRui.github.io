Summary

A long-running cross-sectional stock-selection research project follows the same set of design principles as the new architecture in DeepSeek's two technical reports, and each side arrived at them independently, under entirely different tasks, scales and motivations. The deepest commonality is "exact recent local + compressed distant global", followed by "cheap scoring → sparse selection → reuse" and "bounded, identity-initialized residual mixing". Conversely, the stock-selection project has several lessons not covered by either report that it can offer foundation model research; and the two reports point to the directions most likely to help it break out of its plateau.

  1. Question 1 (connections): V4.1 summarizes DeepSeek-V4 as "SWA as the local-processing backbone, plus compressed global context" (V4.1 p4). Over a thousand-plus experiments, the stock-selection project independently arrived at the same division of labor: the main model is trained on the full history with equal weights (adding recency weighting or shortening the window did not help, and some variants were significantly worse), the online readout uses only the most recent ~20 matured days (consistently better than longer windows), and the market state is compressed into a single "recent-winner mean" entry. Its dynamic enhancement module is nearly isomorphic to CSA's lightning indexer (score → top-k → exact computation), and bounded replay when state is missing corresponds to V4.1's SWA Bounded Replay.
  2. Question 1 (divergence): DeepSeek uses top-k to save compute. Under low-sample conditions, the stock-selection project found that hard top-k is not the optimal statistical estimator: replacing "positive-IC top-k weighting" with dense ridge gave +0.41pp on the full-period primary metric, and further improvements along this line added +2.18pp cumulatively.
  3. Question 2 (new tricks for foundation models): The three most worth validating are: closed-form ridge readout over a matured window (a kind of short-window fast weights: the window should be short, and the magnitude small and fixed); bounded contrastive extrapolation (per-coordinate clipping, amplifying only positive changes, using only the latest one-step difference; difference extrapolation contributes about 2.3pp); and a distillation transferability rule (when the teacher's improvement is smaller than the student's imitation error, it does not transfer, so teachers should be chosen by student gain).
  4. Question 3 (loop transformer): The project's residual chain is essentially an unrolled iterative refinement: small step sizes work, ×2 over-relaxation works, ×4 hurts, and clipped extrapolation at the end gives an extra jump. mHC's doubly stochastic matrices are closed under multiplication, making them a natural fit for weight-shared loops; CSA2's Full / Reindex / Reuse can be ported directly as cross-iteration KV reuse.
  5. Question 4 (improving the stock-selection project): The plateau's bottleneck lies in the student's learning interface and information content, not in the teacher: students distilled from a dozen-plus teacher variants all fall within a narrow band of about 0.25pp. The three most relevant directions are: OPD-style full-cross-section, head-weighted, reverse-KL distillation (keeping an MSE anchor); choosing teachers by student gain rather than teacher score, plus hidden-state distillation; and V4.1's core lesson that the marginal return of the data and environment pipeline is far higher than that of algorithmic innovation (V4.1 p25).
  6. Caveat: All of the project's evaluation years have already been exposed during development; the confidence intervals of most paired comparisons in this document span zero; analogy is not evidence, and the transfer suggestions in Sections 3–5 are all hypotheses awaiting validation.

1. Background and Evidence Base

1.1 What the stock-selection project is, and where it stands

This is a long-running cross-sectional stock-selection project: every day it scores several thousand stocks across the whole market, with the annualized return of a stratified long-short backtest as the primary metric. Below, "late period" means the three most recent complete evaluation years, and "full period" means all evaluation years. The project has accumulated more than two thousand pre-registered experiments, and its main line falls roughly into three stages:

  • Stage 1: NN backbone and dynamic enhancement. A residual MLP over a thousand-plus features, parameter EMA, a soft penalty on the risk exposure of head holdings, short-horizon labels, replacing sparse selection of candidate factors with dense ridge, and a grid over head-correction strength.
  • Stage 2: matured-window online readout and the residual chain. Short-window ridge readout on randomly projected hidden layers, fund-flow and consensus-expectation readouts, ×2 extrapolation of the ranking head, and style extrapolation, ending in a complex teacher made of trees, a dozen-plus NNs and multiple residual layers.
  • Stage 3: teacher–student. A FiLM student with about 400K parameters learns the teacher's ranking, followed by the "full recipe": a 50/50 blend of the student and a Gaussian-rank-calibrated tree model, plus short-window readout, style extrapolation and clipped difference extrapolation. For the current best configuration, the full recipe beats the bare student by about 6pp.

Figure 1 shows three phenomena that matter for what follows. First, the last large climb of the frontier came from "inference-time" modules such as style extrapolation, difference extrapolation and Gaussian-rank calibration, after which the project entered a plateau. Second, for students distilled from the many Stage 3 teacher variants (heterogeneous experts, lagged experts, multi-horizon experts, experts with different data views, etc.), the full recipes all crowd into a narrow band of about 0.25pp, and the bare students crowd into another narrow band: differences between teachers barely carry over to the students. Third, the full recipe beats the bare student by about 6pp, with the main gains coming from the inference-time modules: tree blending, readout, style and difference extrapolation.

1.3 Method and evidence levels

Evidence on the stock-selection side comes from the project's internal experiment summaries, individual experiment reports and mid-term reviews (not public); evidence on the DeepSeek side comes from the two public technical reports. Every correspondence is tagged with an evidence level:

  • A isomorphic at the mechanism level, with direct empirical evidence on both sides;
  • B same principle, but different implementation, scale or motivation;
  • C loose analogy, for inspiration only, not used as an argument.

Numerical conventions: all performance figures are differences (pp) between two configurations under the same convention; confidence intervals are given only where stated in the text. "Primary metric" means the annualized return of the stratified long-short backtest, and "secondary metric" means the annualized return of the simple long-short backtest.

2. Question 1: How the stock-selection project's tricks / insights relate to the new architectures in the two reports

A component-by-component comparison yields a long list of surface similarities. What is more useful is to identify the design principles both sides rely on: they were each chosen under completely different tasks (next-token prediction vs cross-sectional return ranking), scales (trillions vs a few hundred thousand parameters) and motivations (compute and memory vs statistical efficiency), so they are more likely to be general regularities. Below we first give the overview chart and the summary table, then expand on seven principles, and finally explain where the analogy breaks down.

2.2 Seven shared principles

Principle 1: exact recent local + compressed distant global

DeepSeek-V4's three attention types cover exactly three scales: SWA looks exactly at the most recent 128 tokens; CSA compresses every 4 tokens into one entry and attends only to the top-k; HCA compresses every 128 tokens into one entry and attends densely. V4.1 goes further, concluding that the local branch is the real backbone and that the compressed global branch can be greatly simplified (V4.1 p4).

The stock-selection project arrived at the same division of labor along the time axis, and was pushed there step by step by experiments. The main model's weights act as a compressed memory of the entire history: an equal-weight expanding window is the most stable, while recency weighting or shorter windows did not help, and some were significantly worse (one half-life-weighting experiment was 1.35pp below its matched control with a CI excluding zero; training only on the last three years was 3.9pp lower over the full period). Adding even earlier data did not help either, so the rule is not "more history is better" but "the main model should not lean toward the recent past". The online readout acts as an exact local window: about 20 matured days beats 60 and 120 days, a result replicated across several readout families, including fund flow, consensus expectations and ranking readouts. Cross-sectionally, the market context compresses the whole market into a single global entry, the "recent-winner mean".

Why should the local window be short? In non-stationary data, the residual structure the main model has not yet explained drifts quickly: a longer window lowers estimation variance but raises bias, and a ridge over about 20 days sits close to that balance point. The difference between the two sides lies in where information is stored: in the stock-selection project, distant information lives in the weights (parametric memory) and recent information in a closed-form readout (non-parametric); in DeepSeek, both live in the KV. This difference is the starting point for several transfer proposals in Sections 3 and 4.

Principle 2: scoring → selection → reuse

CSA is two-stage: a cheap lightning indexer (low-rank, ReLU, FP4) first scores all compressed entries, and the expensive core attention then runs only on the top-k. V4.1 adds another layer: the first Full layer builds a candidate pool from per-block maximum scores, subsequent Reindex layers re-score only within the pool, and Reuse layers directly reuse the top-k (p10–12).

The stock-selection project's dynamic enhancement is the same pattern in factor space: the scoring function is the mean residual IC over about 20 matured days, "keep only positive IC" plays the role of ReLU, the top-k is drawn separately from a curated pool and the full pool, and the candidate pool is a curated subset screened offline. The market context is the same pattern along the stock dimension: score by recent returns, select the Top-k, aggregate by mean, i.e. sparse attention with uniform weights.

There is one noteworthy divergence between the two. For DeepSeek, top-k selection is an approximation under compute constraints; the stock-selection project found that under low-sample conditions (each score depends on only about 20 cross-sections), hard top-k is itself not a good statistical estimator: replacing "positive-IC Top-k weighting" with a signed ridge over the entire curated pool gained +0.41pp on the full-period primary metric; continuing along this dense ridge route by tuning the correction strength and switching to learning short-horizon returns directly yielded a cumulative +2.18pp. The implication for language models: when indexer scores are very noisy (early in training, long-tail queries), the loss from hard top-k may go beyond a compute approximation; V4.1's "candidate pool widens recall, fine re-scoring within the pool" is precisely a compromise between the two.

On reuse, the stock-selection project's multiple readout heads share one Gram matrix, and the cache stores only first-stage results independent of the main score, while candidate residuals and rolling IC, which depend on the main score, must be recomputed. This is the same engineering principle as CSA2's "share the main KV and indexer K (key side), compute indexer Q (query side) per layer": cache what is query-independent, recompute what is query-dependent.

Principle 3: constrained residual mixing, and when it pays to step outside the bounds

mHC constrains the residual-stream mixing matrix to doubly stochastic matrices (the Birkhoff polytope): the spectral norm is at most 1 and the set is closed under multiplication, so no matter how many layers are stacked, the signal is never amplified. Unconstrained HC frequently showed numerical instability when stacked deep (DSV4 p8).

Most links in the stock-selection residual chain are convex combinations (e.g. two components weighted 0.25 / 0.75) or small corrections on the order of 0.1 std. The student's FiLM restricts scaling to [1−s, 1+s], zero-initializes the last layer, and is exactly the identity when there is no context, which is the same design as mHC's "sigmoid-bounded + small-init gating".

The stock-selection evidence adds one layer beyond "non-expansive everywhere": several key jumps on the frontier came precisely from controlled overshooting. One was 2× extrapolation of the ranking head (late period +1.15pp), another was reverse extrapolation along the style-explained component (full period +1.16pp); both fall outside the convex hull. Difference extrapolation later contributed about 2.3pp in a subsequent recipe (η from 0 to 1). These gains come from different bases and cannot simply be added. Overshooting has a cost: style extrapolation raised full-period maximum drawdown by about 1.4pp; another combination improved the secondary metric and IC in 4/4 groups, but drawdown rose in all of them; overshooting too far is simply worse (readout ×4, correction strength 0.8, correction range narrowed too much). This suggests a finer design: keep internal mixing non-expansive to ensure deep stability, allow bounded expansion only at a few positions (usually the output end), and limit single-point influence with per-coordinate clipping. The "bounded contrastive extrapolation" and "end-clipped secant extrapolation" in Sections 3 and 4 both stem from this.

Principle 4: bounding is robust estimation for heavy-tailed distributions

DSV4 admits that Anticipatory Routing and the SwiGLU clamp are "effective, but the underlying principle is not yet clear" (p44). The stock-selection project offers a testable explanation: on heavy-tailed, low signal-to-noise data, forward clipping amounts to placing an upper bound on each coordinate's influence (similar to the influence function of the Huber loss), trading a little bias for a large drop in variance. The evidence is that clipping width in Figure 4 has a clear interior optimum: ±1σ is best, ±0.5σ clips away signal, and ±2σ lets in noise; readout size, correction strength and readout multiplier, by contrast, get worse as they grow, with the optimum at the small end of the tested range. Mapping each component to a bounded distribution (Gaussian rank, percentile) before mixing also brings a small gain. The testable corollary for language models: the benefit of a clamp should correlate positively with the tail thickness of that layer's activation distribution; monitoring activation kurtosis during training could predict which layers need a clamp and roughly how many standard deviations the threshold should be.

Principle 5: decouple add-on modules from the backbone

V4.1 replaces MTP with DSpark: the draft module is trained separately on a frozen backbone, and training continues in post-training but gradients do not flow back into the backbone (p13–14). The stock-selection experience agrees: none of the multi-horizon or multi-depth heads used as auxiliary training losses helped, whereas readouts built on frozen hidden states were the growth engine of the second phase. A plausible explanation: joint auxiliary losses compete with the main objective for limited representational capacity and optimization steps (the project's NN trains only one round per year, about 180 steps), while add-on modules on a frozen backbone only use the representation without disturbing the backbone.

Principle 6: distillation, multiple teachers and merging

DSV4 uses multi-teacher OPD to merge domain experts, V4.1 extends this to more than 40 heterogeneous teachers and acknowledges that "the best teacher for each domain may come from a different stage of development" (p32). The third phase of the stock-selection project is the low signal-to-noise version of the same thing: a complex teacher provides denoised targets, and a student learning the teacher's ranking scores about 3.6pp higher than one learning the true labels.

The stock-selection project also provides counter-evidence that neither report discusses: teacher improvements do not automatically pass to the student. One teacher was 0.83pp above the original teacher (CI spans zero), yet the bare student distilled from it gained only 0.09–0.16pp, and the full recipe was actually 0.08–0.55pp lower; the student's retention of the teacher's ranking (RankIC) was only 0.82–0.83. Re-distilling from the student's own historical predictions was 0.70–1.75pp below the matched teacher control.

On merging, DSV4 states explicitly that alignment at the logits level avoids the degradation of weight merging (p33), and V4.1 merges parameters only between RL runs of the same lineage (p27). The stock-selection data agree: parameter averaging of independently trained students got worse, multi-round or EMA parameter averaging along the same trajectory was essentially unchanged, and prediction-level ensembling has consistently worked.

Principle 7: data first, and distilling down to the essentials

V4.1's post-training had "no algorithmic innovation", with gains coming "almost entirely" from data and environments (p6, p25). The stock-selection frontier was likewise driven mainly by information: fast feedback from short-horizon labels, fund-flow and consensus-expectation readouts, market context, and so on. Conversely, a large number of architectural experiments brought no stable improvement: token attention (full period −0.50pp), cross-sectional inducing-token attention (lower primary metric in both years), soft-routing MoE (did not beat the baseline in either year), long-window causal CNN (almost identical to a reversed-order control), ZCA whitening, two-tower, same-day MixUp, gradient projection.

"Distilling down to the essentials" has already happened once in the stock-selection project: the complex teacher was overtaken by a student with a few hundred thousand parameters plus four inference-time modules. This matches V4.1's experience of being better overall after removing HCA, overlapping compression and MTP.

2.3 Where the analogy breaks down

  • Different objectives. An LLM optimizes next-token likelihood, and its training objective is broadly consistent with evaluation; the stock-selection project trains with a weighted MSE on neutralized z-score returns but is evaluated on head performance relative to a benchmark index, a mismatch (the top conclusion of the mid-term review).
  • Signal-to-noise ratio and sample size. The stock-selection project's daily cross-sectional IC is only a few percent, with about 180 update steps per year; an LLM has 30T+ tokens. Many architectural gains only show up with large data and may be drowned out by variance in low signal-to-noise tasks.
  • Non-stationarity. The stock-selection "local window" tracks drift; an LLM's local window mainly reflects the locality of linguistic structure, so the optimal windows on the two sides need not have the same cause.
  • Motivation. Most of DeepSeek's structures are approximations serving compute and memory (top-k, compression, reuse, FP4); their stock-selection counterparts are estimators serving statistical efficiency. The optimal parameters behind the same form may be completely different.
  • Evaluation exposure. The stock-selection evaluation years have been used repeatedly, so every choice carries some overfitting; DeepSeek's numbers come from a single large-scale training run and cannot be verified externally.

3. Question 2: New tricks / insights from the stock-selection project that may work for future foundation model development

This section only includes practices that the stock-selection project supports with direct empirical evidence and that the two reports either do not mention or cover only halfway. For each item we give the stock-selection evidence, a mechanistic explanation, a minimal experiment that could be run on a language model, and the main risks. "Novelty" is a subjective judgment relative to the two reports and common public practice. To emphasize: this evidence comes from a task with low signal-to-noise ratio, strong non-stationarity, and very small sample size; whether it holds for LLMs must be re-validated.

3.1 Ten transferable practices

T1Closed-form ridge readout over a matured window: a short-window form of fast weights

Novelty: medium | Related theory: Mesa layers, TTT-linear, DeltaNet, and other modules that "solve least squares in context"

Method. Randomly project the frozen backbone's hidden states to 64 dimensions; solve ridge in closed form on the most recent W samples whose labels have matured (λ ∝ tr(G)/d, where G is the in-window Gram matrix); add the readout to the main output at a small fixed scale.

Stock-selection evidence. A window of about 20 days beats 60 and 120 days (repeated across several readout families, differences +0.8 to +1.1pp); a scale of 0.1 std beats 0.25 and 0.5 std (−0.47pp, −2.88pp); adaptive reliability shrinkage based on recent errors is actually worse (−0.64pp); a shallow-tree readout is on par with the ridge readout (difference 0.07pp); an exact nearest-neighbor readout is essentially tied with ridge (+0.01pp, CI spans zero, and one seed degenerates); multiple readouts share the same Gram and can be stacked.

Mechanism. The ridge readout is exactly the optimal linear fast weights within the window. The stock-selection project adds three priors on top: the window should be short (to track drift); the scale should be small and fixed (about 20 cross-sections are not enough to reliably estimate an adaptive shrinkage coefficient); λ should be proportional to the Gram trace (scale invariance).

Minimal experiment on LLMs. Add a ridge readout head to a frozen LM for streaming or personalization tasks, e.g., in a continual codebase task, use tokens for which "tests have passed" as matured labels. Compare short, medium, and long W, and fixed α vs adaptive α. Prediction: on non-stationary streams, a short window with a small fixed α wins. Further, on a small model, compare sliding-window ridge as a replacement for or complement to the SWA branch.

Risks. Local dependencies in language are not necessarily "drift" in nature, so the short-window advantage may not hold; the readout requires reliable matured labels, which many settings do not have.

T2Bounded contrastive extrapolation: per-coordinate clipping, positive part only, latest step only

Novelty: medium-high | Related methods: CFG, contrastive decoding (DoLa, Contrastive Decoding), ExPO weight extrapolation, activation steering

Method. Final score = current score + η·clip(current score − previous-period score, ±c), with c in units of the cross-sectional standard deviation.

Stock-selection evidence (late-period primary metric). With η = 0 / 0.5 / 1, the gains relative to η=0 are 0 / +0.83 / +2.30pp; clipping at ±1σ is best, with ±0.5σ and ±2σ at −1.02pp and −0.39pp respectively; adding only the positive part is 0.90pp lower than two-sided, and adding only the negative part is 2.19pp lower; a 1-day difference is best, while 2-day and 5-day means are 0.75pp and 1.55pp lower. Extrapolation along the "style-explained component" direction also works, but raises drawdown.

Relation to existing methods. CFG, contrastive decoding, ExPO, and steering all "extrapolate along some contrastive direction." The stock-selection project adds three constraints that are rare in public practice: per-coordinate clipping (in units of the standard deviation of the difference at that position); asymmetry (amplifying only the positive changes contributes most); and using only the latest one-step difference, without smoothing.

Minimal experiment on LLMs. (a) Apply per-token clipping to the logit difference in CFG or contrastive decoding, and compare clipped vs unclipped on accuracy and tail failure rates (repetition, going off-topic, hallucination); (b) ExPO-style weight extrapolation with element-wise clipping; (c) DoLa-style contrast along the iteration dimension of a looped model (iteration K vs iteration K−1); see L1 in Section 4.

Risks. In the stock-selection project, the gain from difference extrapolation may partly come from market-specific short-term momentum structure and is not necessarily a general rule; extrapolation raises tail risk, so evaluation must look at both the mean and the tail.

T3Calibrate first, then mix

Novelty: low to medium | Common in engineering, but rarely systematically ablated in multi-teacher OPD

Method. Before mixing heterogeneous components, map each component to a cross-sectional Gaussian rank or percentile z, then add them with fixed weights.

Stock-selection evidence. After aggregating multiple NN seeds by percentile first and switching tree models to normal ranks, the late-period primary metric rose by +0.10pp; cross-sectional standardization plus clipping is applied uniformly across the whole pipeline. The effect is small, but it has been kept in every subsequent recipe.

Transfer to LLMs. V4.1's OPD has 40+ teachers with different architectures (p32), whose logit scales and effective temperatures differ greatly; mixing multiple reward models has the same issue. We suggest per-prompt temperature calibration or a rank transform before mixing, with "calibrated or not" as an explicit ablation.

T4Recipe diversity beats seed diversity; parameter averaging requires the same basin

Novelty: medium | Consistent with V4.1's "merging RL runs with different scaffolds or configurations"

Stock-selection evidence. The current best scheme comes from combining training types (1 or 2 training rounds × different input masking rates); different type combinations of nine NNs differ by less than 0.02pp, which is very stable; parameter averaging of independently trained students hurts (−0.44pp, −0.64pp), and multi-round EMA averaging along the same trajectory gives no gain.

Transfer to LLMs. When building ensembles or teacher pools, or merging models, creating diversity through recipe differences (data order, mask rate, number of training epochs, learning-rate schedule) is more effective than just changing seeds; merge parameters only between models that share an initialization and differ little, otherwise ensemble in output space. DSV4 also notes that logit alignment avoids the degradation of weight merging (p33).

T5A rule for distillation transferability

Novelty: medium-high | Both reports assume "a stronger teacher yields a stronger student" and do not discuss when transfer fails

Stock-selection evidence. Learning the teacher's ranking is about 3.6pp better than learning the true labels; but a +0.83pp improvement in one teacher transfers only about 0.1pp (bare student), and the full recipe even declines; the student retains only 0.82–0.83 of the teacher's ranking; students of a dozen or so teacher variants all fall within a narrow band of about 0.25pp; re-distilling from the student's own predictions makes things worse (−0.70 to −1.75pp).

Statement of the rule. When the student's imitation error exceeds the differences between teachers, upgrading the teacher does almost nothing for the student. In that regime one should invest in imitation fidelity (target form, capacity, training epochs) rather than look for stronger teachers; teachers should be chosen by student gain, not by the teacher's own score. V4.1's observation that "the best teacher for each domain may come from an earlier development stage" (p32) is consistent with this rule.

Minimal experiment on LLMs. In multi-teacher OPD, record per domain the "transfer ratio" (student gain divided by teacher gain) and "fidelity" (KL or top-k agreement between student and teacher distributions), and allocate teacher weights wi by transfer ratio rather than teacher benchmark scores; when fidelity is low in a domain, first change the target form or increase the student's training in that domain before considering a different teacher.

T6Keep the main loss full-distribution; use the head only as an auxiliary constraint

Novelty: medium | Consistent with DSV4's reasons for insisting on full-vocabulary OPD

Stock-selection evidence. Making the head the main objective fails catastrophically: replacing the main loss with a binary "in the top 10% or not" classification, relative to an MSE base of the same architecture, gives −27.98pp on the secondary metric and −7.26pp on the primary metric; switching to an upper expectile gives −14.21pp secondary and −4.02pp primary; fitting the readout on head samples only is also slightly worse (−0.25pp). Conversely, adding a 0.1× "soft top-10% exposure penalty" to the MSE main loss gives +2.07pp on the full-period primary metric, with positive lower bounds on both primary intervals.

Correspondence. DSV4 chose full-vocabulary OPD because per-token KL estimates "have high variance and often cause training instability" (p33). What both sides share: sparse, head-only supervision signals have too much variance, and the main loss must be dense.

Transfer to LLMs. Do not replace full-distribution likelihood or KL with sparse objectives such as pass@1 or top-1; if you want to constrain behavior in the high-probability region (e.g., verbosity, repetition, sycophancy), add a small-weight, differentiable "head exposure penalty" on top of the full-distribution main loss.

T7Two-timescale continual learning and update cadence

Novelty: medium | DSV4 lists online learning as the next-generation paradigm (p4, p44)

Stock-selection evidence. The main model uses all history without favoring recent data (principle 1 in Section 2); one mid-year incremental update on newly matured samples with a very small learning rate gives the bare student +2.57pp in the late period; but quarterly updates are no better than semi-annual ones (full recipe full period −0.22pp and −0.44pp), and a third training round gives no gain either.

Transfer to LLMs. In continual pretraining and online learning, the backbone should not be overly biased toward recent data; leave recent adaptation to fast modules. Incremental updates have an optimal cadence; updating too often brings noise and drift into the backbone. Treat "update cadence" as a hyperparameter to sweep, rather than assuming more frequent is better.

T8Maturity discipline for delayed feedback

Novelty: medium | Common sense in finance, rarely systematized in LLM online learning

Stock-selection practice. Signals first enter a pending-settlement queue; only samples whose label endpoint is strictly earlier than the current decision date may enter any statistic; shorter-horizon labels mature faster and give adaptive modules fresher feedback (full period +1.06pp, CI [−0.31, 2.48] spans zero). When dates are non-contiguous, bounded replay is used to rebuild state.

Transfer to LLMs. Long-horizon agent tasks, user feedback, and code tasks with delayed verification all have a "label maturity" problem. Online learning systems should explicitly maintain a maturity queue, split strictly at the maturity boundary during evaluation, and avoid training on outcomes that are not yet observable; fast-maturing proxy signals can drive fast modules, while slow-maturing true signals correct the backbone.

T9Correct only at the head

Novelty: low to medium | Same origin as the "plausibility constraint" in contrastive decoding

Stock-selection practice. The dynamic enhancement only corrects stocks near the top of the base ranking. In grid experiments, correcting the top 50% at strength 0.4 is best; narrowing the range to the top 10% lowers the primary metric by about 3.0pp, and raising strength to 0.6 and 0.8 lowers it by 1.43pp and 3.02pp respectively (full period).

Transfer to LLMs. Corrections from steering, CFG, and reward-guided decoding should act only on the foundation model's top-p set, at moderate strength. This matches contrastive decoding's "correct only candidates with p ≥ α·max p"; the stock-selection data show that both the correction range and strength have a clear optimal interval.

T10A protocol for low signal-to-noise research

Nature: methodology | V4.1's scaling ladder (p20) is the same idea along the training-scale dimension

Stock-selection practice. The same architecture is fixed across all evaluation years, with no picking of per-year winners; three seed groups; 20-day circular block bootstrap (5,000 draws); pre-registered small parameter families; exposure is logged. The mid-term review pointed out that single-point tests are "equivalent to a coin flip," and that screening on only the early period and a single seed is biased.

Quantification. The paired difference between the two adjacent best schemes is +0.17pp, CI [−0.22, 0.57], i.e., the paired CI half-width is about 0.4pp. Improvements smaller than this cannot be distinguished in a single comparison.

Transfer to LLMs. Architecture ablations and benchmark evaluations have the same development-exposure and sample-correlation issues. We suggest block bootstrap over samples from the same task family; computing the minimum detectable effect before running experiments; and logging exposure counts for repeatedly used benchmarks.

4. Question 3: Implications for loop transformer foundation model research

A loop transformer is a model that shares weights and repeatedly executes the same block along the depth direction, e.g., the Universal Transformer, recurrent-depth latent reasoning models, Mixture-of-Recursions, and recursive reasoning models such as HRM / TRM. They face four core problems: whether iterations are stable, when to stop, the KV cost of each iteration, and how to train intermediate iterations. The stock-selection project has a not-so-obvious but apt counterpart: its residual chain is itself an "unrolled iterative refinement."

4.1 The stock-selection residual chain is an unrolled iterative refinement

Each stage of the residual chain can be written as:

score_{k+1} = score_k + α_k · f_k(score_k, features, matured feedback)

The frontier curve (Figure 1) shows the typical signature of fixed-point iteration: diminishing gains at each stage; αk is usually small (0.1–0.4 std); over-relaxation (×2) at a few positions helps; larger amplification (×4) hurts; and a final clipped difference extrapolation along the time direction brings one more jump. In classical numerical analysis, small step sizes guarantee contraction, moderate over-relaxation (SOR) accelerates convergence, and the end can be extrapolated with secant or Anderson-type methods. The stock-selection project retraced this path experimentally.

4.2 Ten design implications

L1Small steps + bounded over-relaxation + clipped secant extrapolation at the end

Design. Each iteration computes hk+1 = hk + αk·F(hk, x), with αk = s·tanh(ak) bounded and ak zero-initialized; after the loop, output hK + η·clip(hK − hK−1, ±c).

Rationale. In the stock-selection project, a small scale is best, ×2 helps while ×4 hurts, and difference clipping at ±1σ is optimal (Figure 4). Testable predictions: end-of-loop extrapolation reaches the same quality with fewer iterations; clipping prevents divergence on individual tokens.

L2mHC's doubly stochastic mixing is a natural fit for weight sharing

Rationale. Doubly stochastic matrices are closed under multiplication: the same B multiplied by itself K times is still doubly stochastic, with spectral norm at most 1, so no number of loops will amplify the signal (DSV4 p8). Unconstrained HC in a loop amounts to raising the same matrix to the K-th power, which is more prone to instability than in an ordinary deep network.

Design. Use mHC inside the loop block to mix multiple residual streams, allowing bounded expansion only at the output (combined with L1). V4.1's Single-Pass mHC has block l use the previous block's mixing coefficients Al−1 (p13); this is even more natural in a loop: iteration k uses the coefficients from iteration k−1, which enables pipelining and matches the stock-selection practice that "all gates are computed from lagged data."

L3Cross-iteration KV reuse: moving CSA2 and CED to the iteration dimension

Problem. Looping K times usually produces K copies of KV, which is the main source of inference cost for loop transformers.

Design. The first iteration computes KV and the indexer in Full mode; later iterations use Reindex (shared KV, with the current iteration's indexer Q re-selecting top-k within the candidate pool) or Reuse (reusing top-k as well). The CED idea is: the encoder computes the global KV only once, only the decoder loops, and each iteration keeps its own SWA local state.

Stock-selection counterpart. Multiple readout heads share the same Gram (key side) and only recompute their own targets (query side), with no loss in performance. Testable predictions: attention targets are highly similar across iterations, Reindex is sufficient to capture changes in selection between iterations, and KV cost can drop from linear in K to nearly constant.

L4Train exit and confidence heads separately on frozen loop states

Rationale. In the stock-selection project, jointly trained auxiliary heads did not help while readouts on frozen hidden states did; V4.1 replaces MTP with DSpark trained on a frozen backbone.

Design. First train the looped backbone, then freeze it and train a "continue iterating or not" confidence head on the states of each iteration, so that auxiliary losses do not interfere with the backbone's representations.

L5Control the number of loops with an effort scalar

Design. Borrow V4.1's reasoning effort b and exponential penalty k(b) = k0·exp(−(b − bmin)/τ), applying the penalty to the number of iterations rather than the number of tokens, so that a single model covers a continuous compute budget.

Testable prediction. Front-loaded gains: both the stock-selection strength knob and V4.1's reasoning effort get most of the benefit at low-to-medium settings (V4.1 p34).

L6Distillation from a pipeline into a loop

Rationale. In the stock-selection project, the full recipe is about 6pp above the bare student, and the gain comes from several stages of inference-time refinement; a complex teacher's multi-stage residual chain naturally provides stage-by-stage intermediate targets.

Design. Use the output of each stage of the teacher pipeline to supervise the k-th loop iteration (stage-wise deep supervision), so that a small loop block learns to reproduce multi-stage refinement. In LLMs, a "progressively improving sequence of teachers" (e.g., the same teacher's outputs at different reasoning efforts) can supervise different iteration depths.

L7Closed-form ridge within iterations (fast weights)

Rationale. The most reliable adaptive module in the stock-selection project is the short-window closed-form ridge; theoretical work shows that transformers can perform gradient descent or least squares in context (the series of works by von Oswald et al.).

Design. Each iteration performs one step of in-context least squares or conjugate gradient, with a short window and λ proportional to the Gram trace; the number of loops corresponds to the number of optimization steps. This gives an analyzable version of "why looping helps."

L8Cross-depth ensembling

Rationale. In the stock-selection project, mixing students trained for 1 and 2 rounds beats either one alone.

Design. Average the predictions of outputs at K = 4, 6, 8 iterations, or weight them by exit-head confidence, gaining diversity at very low cost.

L9Contraction and "overthinking"

Rationale. In the stock-selection project, a third training round, quarterly updates, and ×4 amplification were all no better: more iterations are not monotonically beneficial.

Design. Monitor whether the iteration residual ‖hk+1 − hk‖ decreases monotonically as a diagnostic of fixed-point convergence; in the RL stage, use a V4.1-style iteration penalty to suppress excessive iteration.

L10Fixed routing within iterations

Rationale. DSV4's Anticipatory Routing computes routing with lagged parameters to stabilize training (p26); CSA2's Reuse directly reuses top-k; all selections in the stock-selection project are based on lagged, smoothed statistics.

Design. MoE routing in the loop is decided at the first iteration, and later iterations reuse it or allow only small changes, which both stabilizes training and makes it easier to prefetch experts.

5. Question 4: Tricks / insights from the two new models for further improving the stock-selection project

5.1 Diagnose first: what the plateau tells us

  • The post-processing route is essentially saturated. About 1,100 experiments were run after the last big climb, and the late-period primary metric gained only about 0.25pp in total.
  • The teacher is not the bottleneck. More than a dozen teachers—multi-horizon residual teachers, lagged heterogeneous experts, tree/MLP/GRU combinations, MLPs with different history lengths, experts on different data views—all yield distilled students whose full recipe falls within a narrow band of about 0.25pp; averaging multiple teachers and training-time auxiliary heads also stay inside this band.
  • The student's learning interface is the bottleneck. The student retains only 0.82–0.83 of the teacher's ranking; of the teacher's +0.83pp, only about 0.1pp is passed on. Widening the student's hidden layer from 128 to 192 to 256 changes results by less than 0.2pp, so capacity is not the issue either. Learning from the teacher beats learning from true labels by about 3.6pp, showing that "what to learn and how to learn it" matters far more than "how big the model is".
  • Statistical resolution is insufficient to support fine-tuning. The gap to the internal target is 0.87pp, about 2× the half-width of the paired interval (≈0.4pp). Changes on the order of ±0.2pp cannot be confirmed under the current evaluation framework; ideas worth running should have a mechanism supporting an effect of ≥1pp, or several independent small improvements should be pre-registered and tested jointly as one bundle.
  • The information sources have not expanded, and this is exactly the direction V4.1 considers to have the highest return.
In one sentence: what the project lacks now is not a better teacher or more post-processing, but how much the student can learn from the teacher (the learning interface) and how much new information the system can see (data). The two reports happen to offer the most direct lessons on exactly these two points: the objective design of OPD, and data first.

5.3 Concrete plans for the top three

P1OPD-style cross-sectional distillation

For the tradable stock set S_t on each trading day t (z_T is the teacher score, z_S the student score, both cross-sectional z-scores): Long side p_T = softmax(z_T / τ) p_S = softmax(z_S / τ) Short side q_T = softmax(−z_T / τ) q_S = softmax(−z_S / τ) L = MSE(z_S, z_T) + λ·risk penalty + β·[ KL(p_S ‖ p_T) + KL(q_S ‖ q_T) ] on-policy version: the KL terms are further weighted by the student's current top/bottom-decile membership (stop-gradient)

Key settings. τ is set (fixed in advance) so that the teacher's top 10% of stocks hold about 70% of the probability mass; β takes two levels, 0.1 and 0.3; batches are grouped by trading day, each containing the full cross-sections of a few trading days. Student architecture, epochs and mid-year updates stay as in the current best setup.

Why it might work. First, the current MSE treats the middle 80% of the cross-section the same as the tails, while the primary metric only looks at the tails; reverse KL is mode-seeking and will prioritize fitting the teacher's head. Second, earlier ranking or head objectives on true labels all failed, but true labels are very noisy; the teacher target is already denoised, and DSV4's experience is likewise that full-distribution KL on the teacher distribution is what makes training stable. Third, keeping the MSE anchor avoids the variance explosion of looking only at the head.

Criteria. Besides the primary metric, also report the RankIC between student and teacher within the top/bottom deciles (fidelity), and test whether the transfer ratio rises: train one student each from two teachers about 0.83pp apart, and see whether more of that gap passes to the student. If fidelity rises but the primary metric does not, the bottleneck has shifted to the teacher or to the amount of information.

P2Data first

New sources. Provided they are available and auditable point-in-time, prioritize information orthogonal to existing features, money flow and consensus forecasts—for example margin trading and short selling, changes in foreign holdings, event features from announcement and news text, detailed analyst revisions, and option-implied information (depending on coverage).

How to plug in. First plug them in as an independent short-window matured readout. This is the most reliable integration path in the project's history: readouts such as money flow and consensus forecasts all produced gains when layered on a strong base, while feeding new sources directly into the main network's input had limited effect. Only after an increment is confirmed, consider adding them to the student's input.

Hidden duplicates. Prune features by "information increment after residualizing on the main score". In one controlled comparison, the full recipe with about half the features pruned was 0.44pp higher than the unpruned one, showing there is removable redundancy.

scaling ladder. Use small-scale proxies (shorter period, smaller student, single seed) to predict full-scale effects: first check rank agreement between proxy and full scale on existing experiments, and once agreement is sufficient, use the proxy to screen data changes. This mirrors V4.1's practice (p20) and is a direct fix for the retrospective finding that "the screening protocol is biased".

P3Select teachers by student gain, plus hidden-state distillation

Select teachers by student gain. The teacher pool already has multiple teachers. Predefine a few market states (e.g. high or low cross-sectional return dispersion, strong or weak size style, using only data available the day before the decision), and within each state choose teachers or assign weights by the student gain over the matured window rather than the teacher's own primary metric; decide once a year, with no daily adaptation.

Hidden-state distillation. To support a large number of teachers, DSV4 caches only the teachers' last-layer hidden states and reconstructs logits on the fly during training (p34). The teacher's last-layer hidden states and their fixed random projections are already used in readouts, and can directly serve as an auxiliary target for the student: after a linear mapping, the student's last layer is aligned with the teacher's low-dimensional projection (small-weight MSE). This gives the student far richer supervision than a single ranking score, directly targeting the "retention of only 0.82" bottleneck.

5.4 Things not to keep doing

  • Producing more teacher variants. With the learning interface unchanged, differences between teachers do not reach the student.
  • Tuning post-processing knobs at the ±0.2pp level. At the current statistical resolution, real and spurious effects cannot be told apart.
  • Daily adaptive weights or confidence gating. Fixed weights have won repeatedly in the past.
  • Using head metrics directly as the main loss. Binary classification and upper-tail expectile objectives both degraded badly.
  • Large-multiple extrapolation. ×4 and strength 0.8 have been falsified, and extrapolation also pushes up drawdown.
  • Replacing the backbone architecture without new information. Token attention, MoE, GRU, two-tower, whitening and the like have historically been negative or flat.
All suggestions should be run under the protocol of "the same architecture fixed across all evaluation years, three seed groups, block bootstrap, strict maturity boundaries"; new data sources must undergo point-in-time audit. Since all evaluation years have already been exposed, any newer data should be held out as a genuine forward test set.

6. Limitations and caveats

  • Exposure and selection bias. All evaluation years of the stock-selection project have been used repeatedly for development and selection, so any "target reached" result contains selection bias. Most gains cited here are point estimates.
  • Confidence intervals. In most paired comparisons the CI spans zero; e.g. the fast-feedback improvement is [−0.31, 2.48], and the difference between the two adjacent best setups is [−0.22, 0.57]. A ranking of point estimates is not a reliable difference.
  • Analogy is not evidence. The correspondences in Section 2 show that both sides use the same design principles; they do not show that porting a given DeepSeek component to the stock-selection project will work, nor the reverse. The suggestions in Sections 3–5 are all hypotheses to be tested.
  • DeepSeek's numbers are not reproduced. KV compression ratios, FLOPs, benchmark scores, etc. all come from the reports themselves. Page numbers are PDF page numbers, which match the printed page numbers of both reports.
  • Redaction. For public release, this article removes absolute performance levels, capital size, market and data sources, internal identifiers and system details; some numbers are rounded (e.g. "about 0.25pp", "about 6pp"). The removed information does not affect the methodological conclusions.

C. Terminology

Primary / secondary metricAnnualized return of the tiered long-short backtest / simple long-short backtest. This article reports only differences between two configurations.
Late period / full periodThe most recent three complete evaluation years / all evaluation years.
MaturedThe future-return interval needed for the label has ended; a label with an h-day horizon needs h+1 trading days to mature.
ReadoutA ridge correction term fitted in closed form over the most recent matured days on the outputs or hidden states of a frozen model.
Full recipe / bare studentHalf student NN and half Gaussian-rank-calibrated tree model, plus short-window readouts, style extrapolation and clipped difference extrapolation; the student blend without these modules is called the bare student.
RetentionCross-sectional rank correlation (RankIC) between student and teacher predictions.
CSA / HCA / SWACompressed sparse attention / heavily compressed attention / sliding-window attention.
mHCManifold-constrained hyper-connections: multiple residual streams, with the mixing matrix constrained to be doubly stochastic.
CED / CSA2Causal encoder–decoder / second-generation compressed sparse attention (reusing KV, indexer K and top-k across layers).
OPDon-policy distillation: the student aligns with the teacher distribution on sequences it generates itself.
DSpark / EngramV4.1's parallel drafting module / hashed n-gram conditional memory.
